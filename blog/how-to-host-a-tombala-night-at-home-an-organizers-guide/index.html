How to Host a Tombala Night at Home: An Organizer's Guide

By Nesim Razon, independent mobile developer · 2026-11-25

#Game design

Tombala is the Turkish bingo-like number game played with 90 numbers and cards of three rows and nine columns. It suits a family evening or a gathering of friends, but a good night depends on the person running it. This guide covers what to prepare, how to run the draw, how to settle the prizes, and how to keep everybody happy. It is all for fun: no money is staked at any point.

Decide the size of the evening

Four to ten players is comfortable. With fewer, rounds feel quiet; with more, the organizer struggles to check claims and the draw drags on. Give each player one card to start with, or two if everybody knows the game. Three cards at once is the most a person can watch without losing track, and it is also the most the Tombalings app allows.

Cards matter more than their count. A set of six cards can cover every number from 1 to 90 exactly once. Our post on how a tombala card is generated explains how such a set is built. In practice this means: if six players hold one card each from the same set, every drawn number lands on exactly one player's card, so nobody sits through a round with nothing to mark. Cards cut at random from different sources can hold the same number many times or miss some entirely, and the round then feels lopsided.

What you need

  • Cards: printed or bought tombala cards with 3 rows and 9 columns, 15 numbers each. Check that no two cards in the room are identical.
  • The draw: numbers 1 to 90, each exactly once. Paper slips folded to the same size in a bowl work well. A cloth bag with 90 numbered tokens works too. A phone app that draws without repeating is fine as long as it never shows a number twice.
  • A master sheet: a page listing 1 to 90 where the organizer crosses out every drawn number. This is your proof when anyone calls.
  • Markers: dry beans, buttons or paper squares are the classic choice. Pens are fine if cards are disposable. A sticker dot per number is tidy for small children.
  • Prizes and a table big enough for every card to lie flat in full view.

Running the draw

Fold, shuffle and check the slips before guests arrive. Count them: 90 slips, no duplicates, no blanks. Pull one at a time, show it to the table, then read it twice, once as a number and once digit by digit, for example forty-seven, four and seven. The second reading helps people who mix up 16 and 60.

Cross the number off the master sheet before you reach for the next slip. Never put a slip back. Leave a short pause, about five or six seconds early on and a little less later, so slower players can find and mark the number. If the table is mixed in age, slow the pace once and watch who is behind.

Calling numbers and confirming marks

Anyone may call when they believe they have a stage. The caller stops the draw, reads out the numbers of the row or card they are claiming, and the organizer ticks each one against the master sheet. Every number must be on the sheet. One missing number means the claim is not valid, and play continues without punishment or teasing.

A good habit is to ask everyone to mark only after hearing the number, never in advance, and to keep the marked cards visible on the table. It cuts down on mistakes, and kids learn faster by watching.

The prizes, in order

  • 1st line (first cinko): all 5 numbers of one row are marked.
  • 2nd line (second cinko): two full rows are marked on the same card.
  • Tombala: all 15 numbers of a card are marked. This ends the round.

The prizes are given in order. Nobody can take the 2nd line before the 1st line has been called, and the Tombala comes last. When a player completes the row they need, they call right away, and the first player to call gets the prize.

Decide two house rules before the first number: can one person win more than one stage in a round, and what happens if two people call at exactly the same moment. A simple answer to the second is to share the prize, or to let each of them pull a slip and the higher number wins. Say it out loud once; it saves an argument later.

Prizes that are not money

Because the evening is for fun, rewards should be small and silly, never cash and never anything that feels like a bet. Ideas that work well:

  • A bowl of the good snacks, picked first by the winner of the stage.
  • A small gift from a shared pile, such as a notebook, a candle or a bar of chocolate.
  • The right to choose the next board game or the next song.
  • A paper crown for the Tombala, worn until the next one.
  • Bragging rights, written on a family scoreboard that stays on the fridge.

Keep the same level of prize for each stage so that the 1st line does not feel like a consolation.

Keeping it friendly for mixed ages

Young children can play with one card and a helper sitting next to them. Older relatives may need larger print or a second look at the card; hold the draw a little longer for them. Give the caller role to a teenager sometimes, since reading numbers aloud is good practice and keeps them involved.

Do not allow teasing about missed numbers. A missed number is the most common thing that happens in tombala, and it should be treated as normal. If a child is clearly upset, let them help with the master sheet for a round.

A 60 to 90 minute schedule

  • First 10 minutes: welcome, hand out cards, explain the three stages and the two house rules.
  • Next 45 to 60 minutes: three or four rounds, each about 12 to 15 minutes. Tell people to stretch between rounds.
  • Last 10 to 15 minutes: a short final round with the best prize, then a thank-you and the crown ceremony.
  • Between rounds: snacks, new cards if you have them, and a quick look at who has not won anything yet.

Common mistakes

  • Duplicate cards: two identical cards in the same game cause pairs of winners and bad feelings. Count and compare them before you start.
  • Missed numbers: the caller forgets to cross a number or a player forgets to mark. Keep the master sheet honest and read slowly.
  • Arguments over ties: unclear rules. Fix them before the first draw.
  • Too many cards per person: more than three means a player watches the cards, not the room.
  • Putting slips back into the bowl: a number comes up twice and the whole round becomes doubtful.
  • Rushing: a fast draw rewards the most experienced player only.

Frequently asked questions

How many cards should each player have?

One is enough for beginners and children. Experienced players can take two or three, but beyond three it gets hard to follow every number.

How can I draw the numbers fairly at home?

Use 90 equal paper slips in a bowl or 90 tokens in a bag, draw one at a time without putting it back, and cross each number off a master sheet.

What if two people call at the same time?

Agree a rule before the game starts, such as sharing the prize or each pulling a slip with the higher number winning.

Can we play tombala for money?

This guide is for friendly play only. Keep prizes small and fun, such as snacks, a small gift or choosing the next game, and stake no money.

How long does one round take?

About 12 to 15 minutes with five to ten players, so three or four rounds fit in a 60 to 90 minute evening.
