Who Sees the New Year First and Last: UTC+14 to UTC-12

By Nesim Razon, independent mobile developer · 2026-11-25

#Dev notes#Algorithms

On the night of December 31st the new year does not arrive at one moment. It walks around the planet over about 26 hours, one zone after another. Here is the order of midnights for the turn from 2026 to 2027, with real offsets checked against the time zone database, and how the New Year countdown in our app Snowlings turns all of this into a single number.

Why midnight is a local event

The Earth turns, so the sun is overhead in different places at different times. To keep clocks sensible, the world is cut into time zones, each defined as a fixed distance from UTC, the shared reference time. A zone at UTC+3 has a clock three hours ahead of UTC, a zone at UTC-5 has a clock five hours behind.

Midnight is simply the moment a local clock reads 00:00 on January 1st. Because the clocks differ, that moment is a different instant for each zone. The same instant on a global timeline can be the first minutes of 2027 in one country and the late afternoon of December 31st in another. A countdown is therefore never just a number of days. It always needs to know whose midnight it is counting toward.

The first to arrive: UTC+14

The earliest clock in the world runs at UTC+14. I checked it with the tz database (version 2026f, through Python's zoneinfo module): Pacific/Kiritimati, in the Line Islands of Kiribati, has an offset of +14:00 on January 1st, 2027, with no daylight saving. Its midnight falls at 10:00 UTC on December 31st, 2026.

That is not an old, stable arrangement. The same database says Kiritimati was at UTC-10 on December 30th, 1994 and at UTC+14 on January 2nd, 1995, a jump of a full day. The islands moved across the date line so that the whole country shares the same calendar day. Samoa did something similar in 2011: Pacific/Apia is at UTC-10 on December 29th, 2011 and at UTC+14 on December 31st, 2011, including its summer time at that point. Today Apia is at UTC+13.

Right next to the first place sits a useful comparison. At the instant Kiritimati rings in 2027, Pacific/Honolulu (UTC-10) is only starting its own December 31st at 00:00. Two places on the same planet are a whole calendar day apart.

The order of midnights

Here is the order for the 2026/2027 turn, with the UTC moment of each local midnight, all computed from the database:

  • 10:00 UTC, December 31st: Pacific/Kiritimati (+14).
  • 10:15 UTC: Pacific/Chatham (+13:45 in summer time).
  • 11:00 UTC: Pacific/Auckland (+13, summer time), Pacific/Tongatapu (+13), Pacific/Apia (+13).
  • 12:00 UTC: Pacific/Fiji (+12).
  • 13:00 UTC: Australia/Sydney (+11, summer time).
  • 18:15 UTC: Asia/Kathmandu (+5:45).
  • 18:30 UTC: Asia/Kolkata (+5:30).
  • 21:00 UTC: Europe/Istanbul (+3).
  • 05:00 UTC, January 1st: America/New_York (-5).
  • 10:00 UTC: Pacific/Honolulu (-10).
  • 11:00 UTC: Pacific/Pago_Pago (-11) and Pacific/Niue (-11).
  • 12:00 UTC: Etc/GMT+12 (-12).

Notice that UTC midnight itself, 00:00 on January 1st, falls in the middle of the list. Western Europe is only reached after most of Asia has already celebrated.

The last to arrive: UTC-12 and UTC-11

The latest clock is UTC-12. In the database it exists as Etc/GMT+12, a generic zone that is not tied to a city. I could not confirm from the data files which islands use it or whether anyone lives there, so I will not say more about them than this: it is the end of the list. Its midnight is at 12:00 UTC on January 1st.

Among zones tied to real places, the late arrivals are at UTC-11, such as Pacific/Pago_Pago and Pacific/Niue, whose midnight is at 11:00 UTC. The gap from the first midnight (10:00 UTC on December 31st) to the last (12:00 UTC on January 1st) is 26 hours. Add one more day, because the first zone is still on January 1st when the last one reaches it, and the calendar date January 1st exists somewhere on Earth for a full 50 hours.

Half hours and quarter hours

Not every offset is a whole number of hours. India uses UTC+5:30 (Asia/Kolkata), and Nepal uses UTC+5:45 (Asia/Kathmandu). That makes Nepal's midnight arrive at 18:15 UTC, fifteen minutes before India's at 18:30 UTC, even though both are in south Asia.

The Chatham Islands (Pacific/Chatham) are the best example. In the southern winter, on July 1st, 2026, their offset is +12:45. In the summer, on January 1st, 2027, it is +13:45. So the Chatham Islands reach the new year 45 minutes before mainland New Zealand's clock, and only 15 minutes after Kiritimati. When someone says that New Zealand is first, that is a rounding of the story.

Summer time in the southern hemisphere

Daylight saving works the other way round south of the equator: clocks go forward in the local summer, around the New Year. The database confirms it. Pacific/Auckland is at UTC+12 on July 1st, 2026 (NZST) and at UTC+13 on January 1st, 2027 (NZDT). Australia/Sydney is at UTC+11 on January 1st, 2027 (AEDT), which is its summer time.

Fiji is a warning against writing offsets into code. Pacific/Fiji has no daylight saving in the data I used: it is at UTC+12 both on December 1st, 2026 and on January 15th, 2027. Tonga and Samoa are at UTC+13 without summer time. These rules are decided by governments and change from time to time. The safe approach is to read them from the time zone database of the operating system, not to remember them.

How Snowlings computes the countdown

In the New Year calendar app Snowlings the countdown on the calendar screen takes the current time in milliseconds and the time zone ID reported by the phone (the Android TimeZone default, the iOS local time zone name; if the ID cannot be used, the app falls back to UTC). It finds the local date, builds January 1st of the next year at the start of the day in that zone, and subtracts. Because the start of the day is computed by the time zone rules, summer time is handled for free: the arithmetic never assumes that a day has 24 hours. The screen recalculates once per second and shows days, hours, minutes and seconds.

A separate midnight screen counts down the same way, updating four times a second, with a large number for the last ten seconds and fireworks when the time is reached. Beneath it the app lists cities that have already entered the new year and the next five. This list works differently from the countdown. It is a fixed list of 22 cities, from Kiritimati at +14 to Honolulu at -10, each with a stored offset chosen for the December and January period, and a city's midnight is January 1st 00:00 UTC minus that offset. That is simple and exact for one night a year, and it needs no zone database. The price is that these offsets have to be checked by hand if a country changes its rules. The app is Snowlings, if you want to watch the walk around the planet in real time.

Frequently asked questions

Which time zone sees the New Year first?

UTC+14. In the tz database it is used by Pacific/Kiritimati in Kiribati's Line Islands, whose midnight is at 10:00 UTC on December 31st.

Why do some zones differ by 30 or 45 minutes?

Governments choose offsets freely. India is at UTC+5:30, Nepal at UTC+5:45, and the Chatham Islands at +12:45 in winter and +13:45 in summer.

How long does the New Year take to travel around the world?

From the first midnight (UTC+14) to the last (UTC-12) it takes 26 hours.

Does daylight saving change when the New Year arrives?

Yes. Auckland is at UTC+13 in January and UTC+12 in July, so its midnight moves by one hour depending on the rule. Read the offset from the time zone database.

How does a countdown handle different time zones?

It computes January 1st at 00:00 in the device's time zone and subtracts the current time, so each device counts down to its own local midnight.
